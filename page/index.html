Abstract

Evaluation of multimodal reasoning models is typically reduced to a single accuracy score, implicitly treating reasoning as a unitary capability. We introduce MathLens, a benchmark of textbook-style geometry problems that exposes this assumption by operationally decomposing performance into perception, reasoning, and multimodal-specific components. Each problem is derived from a symbolic specification and accompanied by visual diagrams, text-only variants, multimodal questions, and targeted perceptual probes, enabling controlled measurement of each component. Using this decomposition, we show that models trained with common strategies exhibit systematically different capability profiles that are invisible under aggregate accuracy. Models trained with reinforcement learning gain primarily in perceptual grounding and in robustness to diagram variation, while gains after textual SFT coincide with more reflective reasoning. In contrast, as perception and reasoning improve, a growing fraction of remaining errors fall outside these components and are categorized as multimodal-specific. These results suggest that apparent progress in multimodal reasoning reflects shifting balances among subskills rather than uniform advancement, motivating evaluation beyond scalar accuracy.

MathLens

A wrong answer on a diagram problem doesn't tell you whether the model misread the diagram or failed to reason. MathLens pairs each geometry problem with tests that separate the two.

One problem, three tests. Perception: two-option probes on facts in the diagram. Reasoning: the diagram replaced by a complete text description. Multimodal-specific: the full problem fails although both isolated tests pass.

Built from a symbolic state. Text descriptions, probes, and questions come from FormalGeo-7K predicates; diagrams are re-rendered from them.

Finding 1

Multimodal RL gains vary with textual reasoning strength

Textual SFT lifts Revisual-R1 and OVR by 23–27 points on text input. The multimodal RL stage that follows then gains 4–5 points on diagram input. Direct multimodal RL gains are modest in both.

Accuracy change (points) on text vs. diagram input. Light bars: not significant.

Finding 2

Perception improves after textual SFT, which uses no images

Textual SFT trains on no images, yet raises perception-probe accuracy by 3–6 points. Every multimodal RL stage raises it too; multimodal SFT lowers it. Probe answers that turn correct contain more reflection than ones that were already correct.

Perception-probe accuracy change (points).

Reflections per answer: already correct (true→true) vs. newly correct (false→true). Rows: Revisual-R1, OVR.

Finding 3

Perception and reasoning errors fall together; multimodal-specific errors grow

For Revisual-R1 and OVR, the share of answers failing both isolated tests drops from 25% to 7%. The share failing only the full problem (multimodal-specific) rises from 9% to 15–17%.

Answers by error type. “Interaction” is the multimodal-specific category.

Finding 4

Multimodal RL checkpoints answer more consistently across diagram variants

Consistency, how often a model gives the same answer across variants of one diagram, rises after every multimodal RL stage (Vision-R1: 21% → 39%). Multimodal SFT lowers it and loses accuracy on newly rendered diagrams.

Boxes: accuracy on modified diagrams. ○ accuracy on base diagrams. ◇ consistency rate (agreement across variants of one diagram).

Accuracy change (points) on curated vs. newly rendered diagrams. Light bars: not significant.

Finding 5

Perception gains are uneven

Cocircular, parallel, and collinear probes improve across recipes. Angle and polygon probes improve only with textual SFT → RL. Length and perpendicular probes stay hard.
