What MLLMs Learn about When they Learn about Multimodal Reasoning

Jiwan Chung1,2*, Neel Joshi1, Pratyusha Sharma1, Youngjae Yu3†, Vibhav Vineet1†

1Microsoft Research AI Frontiers  ·  2Yonsei University  ·  3Seoul National University

*Work done while interning at Microsoft Research  ·  †Co-principal investigators

arXiv BibTeX

Multimodal reasoning models are usually compared by one accuracy score. MathLens is a benchmark of 926 textbook-style geometry problems that splits that score into perception, reasoning, and a multimodal-specific residual. Every problem is derived from a symbolic specification, so its diagram, its text-only version, its question, and its perception probes all state exactly the same facts. Comparing released multimodal reasoning models with their backbones, we find that models trained with different recipes have distinct capability profiles that aggregate accuracy does not show.

Abstract

Evaluation of multimodal reasoning models is typically reduced to a single accuracy score, implicitly treating reasoning as a unitary capability. We introduce MathLens, a benchmark of textbook-style geometry problems that exposes this assumption by operationally decomposing performance into perception, reasoning, and multimodal-specific components. Each problem is derived from a symbolic specification and accompanied by visual diagrams, text-only variants, multimodal questions, and targeted perceptual probes, enabling controlled measurement of each component. Using this decomposition, we show that models trained with common strategies exhibit systematically different capability profiles that are invisible under aggregate accuracy. Models trained with reinforcement learning gain primarily in perceptual grounding and in robustness to diagram variation, while gains after textual SFT coincide with more reflective reasoning. In contrast, as perception and reasoning improve, a growing fraction of remaining errors fall outside these components and are categorized as multimodal-specific. These results suggest that apparent progress in multimodal reasoning reflects shifting balances among subskills rather than uniform advancement, motivating evaluation beyond scalar accuracy.

One accuracy score, three sources of error

Most multimodal reasoning benchmarks report only aggregate accuracy. That hides whether an error comes from perception (extracting information from the input), from reasoning (operating on the extracted information), or from their interaction. MathLens is designed to probe these components separately while keeping realistic problem contexts.

For each problem \(k\), MathLens has access to its semantic state \(S_k = \{s_{k,1}, \ldots, s_{k,m}\}\), a set of atomic facts such as \(\angle ABC = 50^\circ\), and a query operator \(\varphi_k\) such as “compute \(\angle A\)”. The diagram, the text description, the question, and the probes are all generated from \(S_k\). That makes three tests possible:

Perception
Each atom \(s_{k,i}\) becomes a probe question whose gold answer is the atom’s valuation. The probes check whether the model can recover, from the diagram, the specific facts needed to solve the problem. Errors here indicate perceptual failures.
Reasoning
A text description that directly encodes the relevant details replaces the diagram. This trivializes perception, so the task reduces to applying \(\varphi_k\) correctly. Errors here isolate reasoning competence, free from perceptual confounds.
Multimodal-specific
Conditional on success in the perception probes and in text-only reasoning, any remaining error on the full diagram task is a multimodal-specific residual. The category is defined operationally, as what is left after controlling for the measured perception and reasoning components.

Every view is generated from one symbolic state

Each instance is first specified symbolically, as \(S_k\) and \(\varphi_k\), and then rendered into its observable forms: diagrams, textual descriptions, and several types of questions. The problems match the domain and complexity of popular benchmarks such as MathVista and MathVerse.

  1. aData source. FormalGeo-7K provides symbolic annotations for geometry problems. Each diagram cue or condition is a predicate, e.g. Collinear(AB,BC) or EqualLength(AB,CD), and together they form \(S_k\).
  2. bQuestions. A question is the clauses \([S_k^{\mathrm{q}}; \varphi_k]\). Clauses that overlap with the diagram context are dropped, so the question cannot be answered without the diagram.
  3. cText descriptions. A template maps each atom to a clause, e.g. EqualLength(AB,CD) → “Segment AB is equal in length to segment CD”: a faithful rendering of \(S_k\) without stylistic variation.
  4. dPerception probes. Each atom becomes a probe, e.g. Collinear(AB,BC) → “Which points are collinear? A. ABC B. ABD”, with answer A and negative B.
  5. eDiagram rendering. Each clause becomes a geometric constraint, e.g. Perpendicular(AB,BC) → \((x_a - x_b)(x_c - x_b) + (y_a - y_b)(y_c - y_b) = 0\). A numerical solver finds coordinates that satisfy all constraints, and the result is rendered and manually filtered to remove artifacts such as occlusions or overlaps.
  6. +Diagram modifications. Altering \(S_k\) while preserving the answer gives semantic variants: added auxiliary lines or shapes, flips, rotations, merged instances, and relabeled points. All variants are manually screened.

What changes after multimodal reasoning training

We evaluate seven open-weight model families in the 7–9B range, 13 checkpoints in total, grouped by training recipe: direct multimodal RL (VL-Rethinker, ShareVL-R1), multimodal SFT followed by multimodal RL (R1-OneVision, Vision-R1), and textual SFT followed by multimodal RL (Revisual-R1, OVR), each next to its Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone. GLM-4.1V-Thinking is outside these categories because its training data is undisclosed. Qwen2.5-VL-72B, VL-Rethinker-72B, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude 4 Sonnet, and GPT-4o/o3 serve as larger and closed reference points.

All comparisons are between released checkpoints and their backbones. Changes between stages carry paired bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over problem instances. Across eight models, MathLens accuracy correlates with MathVista (Spearman’s \(\rho = 0.83\)) and MathVerse (\(\rho = 0.86\)), and more weakly with MathVision (\(\rho = 0.67\)).

Finding 1 · Text versus diagram input

The text description and the diagram encode identical information for the same question, so comparing the two inputs separates textual reasoning from everything else. Textual SFT raises accuracy mostly on text input (+23.4 and +27.3 points). The multimodal RL stage that follows gains on diagram input (+4.7 and +4.2). Direct multimodal RL without textual SFT gains only modestly on both inputs. Multimodal SFT lowers diagram-input accuracy in both families, and text-input accuracy significantly only for Vision-R1.

Finding 1

Gains from multimodal RL vary with textual reasoning strength. After strong textual SFT they appear mainly in perception; without it, they are modest in both modalities.

Finding 2 · Perception and reflection

Perception probes measure low-level recovery of the diagram’s facts. Every multimodal RL checkpoint scores higher on the probes than the stage before it, although RL rewards only correct downstream answers. Textual SFT uses no visual input, yet the textual-SFT checkpoints also do better on the probes (+3.2 and +5.9 points).

We hypothesize that one contributing factor is reflection: stronger reasoning lets a model revisit and revise an initial perceptual judgment. We count reflection markers such as “wait” in the reasoning trace. Among probe answers of the two textual-SFT families, reflection is more frequent in answers that became correct after training (false→true) than in answers that were correct before and after (true→true). Example 1 shows such a trace.

Finding 2

Textual-SFT checkpoints perceive better, and the probe answers they newly get right involve more reflective reasoning.

Finding 3 · Where the remaining errors go

With the four error types, every wrong answer gets a cause. After RL, perception and reasoning errors fall on the same problems, so the two improve together: most of the reduction is in problems that fail both. Many of the remaining errors are multimodal-specific, and that category grows as the others shrink, e.g. from 9% to 15% of problems for Revisual-R1 and from 9% to 17% for OVR.

Finding 3

Perception and reasoning errors fall together, and a growing share of the remaining errors is multimodal-specific once the other components improve.

Finding 4 · Robustness to diagram changes

Pixel-level perturbations such as blurring often shift predictions because of abnormal appearance rather than semantic change. MathLens instead modifies the geometric specification, so each variant changes the diagram’s structure while keeping the answer. Accuracy on modified diagrams is generally lower than on the base diagrams. To quantify robustness we use the Consistency Rate, the expected agreement of a model’s predictions across variants \(\bar{C}_{k,i}, \bar{C}_{k,j}\) of the same diagram:

\[ \mathrm{CR} = \mathbb{E}_{Q'_k}\Big[\, \mathbb{E}_{i \neq j}\, \mathbf{1}\big[ M(Q'_k, \bar{C}_{k,i}) = M(Q'_k, \bar{C}_{k,j}) \big] \Big]. \]

Consistency is higher after multimodal RL and lower after multimodal SFT. Similar downstream accuracy can also hide reliance on visual familiarity: multimodal SFT checkpoints drop on newly rendered diagrams while staying flat on the original curated ones.

Finding 4

Multimodal-RL checkpoints are more consistent under structural diagram variations; multimodal-SFT checkpoints are less consistent.

Finding 5 · Which perception skills change

Split by relation type, the perception probes change unevenly. Relations that depend on simple local geometric primitives (cocircular, parallel, collinear) improve consistently. Same-angle, angle-value, triangle, and quadrilateral probes improve only when textual SFT is combined with multimodal RL. Same-length, length-value, and perpendicular probes remain difficult, likely because their cues are spatially displaced or visually ambiguous.

Finding 5

Perception gains are uneven. Skills driven by direct geometric cues improve, while probes that rely on symbolic marks or long-range visual associations show limited gains.
