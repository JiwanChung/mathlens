MathLens decomposes multimodal reasoning errors into perception, reasoning, and a residual multimodal-specific category (“Integration”), revealing component-specific shifts after finetuning that are hidden by aggregate accuracy.

Abstract

Evaluation of multimodal reasoning models is typically reduced to a single accuracy score, implicitly treating reasoning as a unitary capability. We introduce MathLens, a benchmark of textbook-style geometry problems that exposes this assumption by operationally decomposing performance into perception, reasoning, and multimodal-specific components. Each problem is derived from a symbolic specification and accompanied by visual diagrams, text-only variants, multimodal questions, and targeted perceptual probes, enabling controlled measurement of each component. Using this decomposition, we show that models trained with common strategies exhibit systematically different capability profiles that are invisible under aggregate accuracy. Models trained with reinforcement learning gain primarily in perceptual grounding and in robustness to diagram variation, while gains after textual SFT coincide with more reflective reasoning. In contrast, as perception and reasoning improve, a growing fraction of remaining errors fall outside these components and are categorized as multimodal-specific. These results suggest that apparent progress in multimodal reasoning reflects shifting balances among subskills rather than uniform advancement, motivating evaluation beyond scalar accuracy.

MathLens

Most multimodal reasoning benchmarks report only aggregate accuracy, obscuring whether errors stem from perception (extracting information from inputs), reasoning (operating on extracted information), or their interaction. MathLens is designed to separately probe these components while preserving realistic problem contexts. It comprises 926 geometry problems, each presented with eight visual modifications and an average of 7.03 visual probes per problem.

Based on MathLens annotations, the joint Multimodal Reasoning Test (1) is decomposed into a Perception Test and a textual Reasoning Test. The Perception Test evaluates questions answerable directly from the diagram, such as reading an annotated angle (\(\angle DFB\) in (2)). The Reasoning Test (3) replaces the diagram with a complete textual description, such that the question can be solved without visual access. Finally, the Multimodal-specific category (4) highlights cases where multimodal reasoning fails even though perception and reasoning, when tested independently, succeed.

Isolating components

Each problem \(k\) is specified by a semantic state \(S_k\), a set of atomic facts such as \(\angle ABC = 50^\circ\), and a query operator \(\varphi_k\). Having access to \(S_k\) allows us to design tests that separately probe each component. Perception: probe questions derived from the atomic facts in \(S_k\) check whether the model can recover the facts needed to solve the problem from the diagram. Reasoning: a textual description that directly encodes the relevant details trivializes perception, so errors isolate reasoning competence. Multimodal-specific: conditional on success in the perception probes and in text-only reasoning, any remaining error on the full task is treated as a multimodal-specific residual.

Data generation

We build on FormalGeo-7K, which encodes each diagram cue or condition as a predicate, e.g. Collinear(AB, BC), forming the semantic state. Questions drop clauses that overlap with the diagram, so they require the visual context. Text descriptions and perception probes are templated from each predicate. Diagrams are rendered by converting predicates into geometric constraints, solving for coordinates, and manually filtering artifacts. Diagram modifications (added lines or shapes, flips, rotations, merged instances, relabeled points) alter the semantic state while preserving the answer.

Sample data generation process in MathLens. From a semantic state representation, we build controlled text descriptions, perception probes, and questions with no overlap with visual content. New diagrams are rendered from the semantic state to avoid visual familiarity effects.

We also construct MathLens-NS, 107 problems from diverse domains that trade the formal semantic control of geometry for broader coverage. Its curation is described in the paper.

Analysis

We compare released multimodal reasoning models with their Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone: direct multimodal RL (VL-Rethinker, ShareVL-R1), multimodal SFT followed by multimodal RL (R1-OneVision, Vision-R1), and textual SFT followed by multimodal RL (Revisual-R1, OVR). Pre–post differences carry paired bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over problem instances.

Performance gains by input modality. Percentage-point shifts from finetuning for text versus diagram inputs, with 95% CIs. Top: direct multimodal RL. Middle: multimodal SFT, then multimodal RL. Bottom: textual SFT, then multimodal RL. Light bars are not significant. Textual SFT mainly raises text-input accuracy (+23.4, +27.3), and the multimodal RL stage after it gains on diagram input (+4.7, +4.2).

Perception performance shifts from finetuning in percentage points. Except for multimodal SFT, all stages show higher perception-probe accuracy, including textual SFT, which uses no visual input.

Sample distribution over reflection count. Reflection is more frequent in false→true cases than in true→true cases. Rows: Revisual-R1 and OVR. Left: backbone → textual SFT. Right: textual SFT → multimodal RL.

Distribution of error types per model. Wrong answers are split by the two isolated tests: Perception & Reasoning (both fail), Perception, Reasoning, and Interaction (multimodal-specific: both succeed in isolation). Most gains come from cases failing in both perception and reasoning. Multimodal-specific errors become more prominent as the other categories improve.

Robustness to semantic-level visual modifications. Box plots show accuracy on modified diagrams. Points report accuracy on the unmodified base diagrams and the consistency rate, the expected agreement of predictions across variants of the same diagram. Consistency is higher after multimodal RL and lower after multimodal SFT.

Finetuning shifts by diagram type for existing versus newly rendered diagrams. Multimodal SFT drops on rendered diagrams while staying flat on curated ones. Light bars are not significant.

Perception probe results by question type. Cocircular, parallel, and collinear relations, which depend on simple local primitives, improve consistently. Angle and polygon relations improve only with textual SFT followed by multimodal RL. Length and perpendicular relations remain difficult, likely due to spatially displaced cues or visually ambiguous annotations.
