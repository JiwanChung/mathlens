Abstract

Evaluation of multimodal reasoning models is typically reduced to a single accuracy score, implicitly treating reasoning as a unitary capability. We introduce MathLens, a benchmark of textbook-style geometry problems that exposes this assumption by operationally decomposing performance into perception, reasoning, and multimodal-specific components. Each problem is derived from a symbolic specification and accompanied by visual diagrams, text-only variants, multimodal questions, and targeted perceptual probes, enabling controlled measurement of each component. Using this decomposition, we show that models trained with common strategies exhibit systematically different capability profiles that are invisible under aggregate accuracy. Models trained with reinforcement learning gain primarily in perceptual grounding and in robustness to diagram variation, while gains after textual SFT coincide with more reflective reasoning. In contrast, as perception and reasoning improve, a growing fraction of remaining errors fall outside these components and are categorized as multimodal-specific. These results suggest that apparent progress in multimodal reasoning reflects shifting balances among subskills rather than uniform advancement, motivating evaluation beyond scalar accuracy.

MathLens

Most multimodal reasoning benchmarks report only aggregate accuracy, which hides whether errors come from perception, reasoning, or their interaction. MathLens is designed to probe these components separately.

The multimodal reasoning test (1) is decomposed into a perception test (2), questions answerable directly from the diagram, and a reasoning test (3), where a complete text description replaces the diagram. The multimodal-specific category (4) covers failures on (1) when (2) and (3) succeed.

From a semantic state representation, MathLens builds text descriptions, perception probes, and questions with no overlap with the visual content. New diagrams are rendered from the semantic state to avoid visual familiarity effects.

Analysis

Finding 1

Multimodal RL gains vary with textual reasoning strength

Textual SFT mainly improves accuracy on text input (+23.4 and +27.3 points for Revisual-R1 and OVR, compared with +7.9 and +7.7 on diagram input). Multimodal RL applied afterwards improves accuracy on diagram input by +4.7 and +4.2 points. Direct multimodal RL, without textual SFT, yields modest gains on both inputs (+2.3 to +4.2 points).

Percentage-point shifts from finetuning on text versus diagram input, with 95% confidence intervals. Lighter bars are not significant.

Finding 2

Perception improves after textual SFT, which uses no visual input

Although textual SFT uses no images, perception-probe accuracy increases by 3.2 and 5.9 points for Revisual-R1 and OVR. All six models also improve on perception probes after multimodal RL, whereas both multimodal SFT models decline (−5.5 and −1.6 points). In Revisual-R1 and OVR, reflection is more frequent in probe answers that become correct after training than in answers that were already correct.

Percentage-point shifts in perception-probe accuracy from finetuning, with 95% confidence intervals.

Distribution of reflection counts in probe answers that were correct before and after training (true→true) and that became correct after training (false→true), for Revisual-R1 (top) and OVR (bottom).

Finding 3

Multimodal-specific errors increase as perception and reasoning improve

In the 7B models, most of the reduction in errors comes from problems on which a model fails both the perception and the reasoning test; for Revisual-R1 and OVR, these fall from 25% to 7% of answers. Over the same training, multimodal-specific errors, where both tests succeed but the full problem fails, rise from 9% to 15% and 17%.

Distribution of error types per model. “Interaction” denotes the multimodal-specific category.

Finding 4

Consistency under diagram modifications increases after multimodal RL

We measure consistency as the agreement of a model's answers across modified versions of the same diagram. Consistency increases after multimodal RL in all six models (e.g., from 21.1% to 39.4% for Vision-R1) and decreases after multimodal SFT (from 31.0% to 24.0% and 21.1%). The multimodal SFT models also lose accuracy on newly rendered diagrams (−3.3 and −6.4 points) while remaining stable on curated ones.

Accuracy on modified diagrams (boxes) and on the unmodified base diagrams (○), and consistency (◇).

Percentage-point shifts from finetuning on curated versus newly rendered diagrams, with 95% confidence intervals. Lighter bars are not significant.

Finding 5

Perception gains are uneven

Collinear relations improve in all six models, and parallel and cocircular relations in five. Angle and polygon relations improve most in Revisual-R1 and OVR, the two models trained with textual SFT followed by multimodal RL, whereas length relations show no consistent improvement.
